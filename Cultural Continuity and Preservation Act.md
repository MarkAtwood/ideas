# Proposed: Cultural Continuity and Preservation Act

By Mark Atwood

## Legislative Proposal

### Section 1. Short Title.

This Act may be cited as the "Cultural Continuity and Preservation Act".

### Section 2. Definitions.

For purposes of this Act:

1. *Qualifying library or archive* – A library, archive, or nonprofit
  educational institution (including its library) that is open to the
  public or available to outside researchers, and which conducts
  reproduction and lending under this Act without purpose of direct or
  indirect commercial advantage.

2. *Controlled digital lending* – The lending of a digitized copy of
  a physical work in a manner that limits each copy to one user at a
  time (or no more simultaneous users than the number of lawfully
  acquired copies owned), and employs technological measures to enforce
  limited lending periods and prevent unauthorized redistribution.

3. *Designated cultural archive* – A qualifying library or archive
  designated by the Librarian of Congress (or other authorized official)
  as a repository for works under this Act, including for receipt of
  deposits under Section 7.

### Section 3. Preservation Reproduction and Migration.

Notwithstanding any exclusive rights under title 17, a qualifying
library or archive may reproduce any copyrighted works, or adapt or
migrate such works to new formats or media, for the purposes of
preservation or scholarly research. This right includes the creation
of backup copies and format-shifted versions as needed to ensure the
work’s continued availability. A qualifying library or archive may
also provide controlled on-site or remote access to these preserved
works for researchers and library patrons, provided that any such
access is limited and secure (for example, available only to
authenticated users for purposes of private study, scholarship, or
research, and not exceeding the access that a physical copy would
allow). Nothing in this section permits the offering of preserved
copies to the general public in a manner that would unreasonably
substitute for commercial availability of the work.

### Section 4. Secure Archival Synchronization and Replication.

Qualifying libraries and archives may, for preservation purposes,
securely share or transfer copies of works in their collections to
other qualifying libraries or archives. It shall not be an
infringement for such institutions to synchronize, mirror, or
replicate collections among themselves in order to create redundant
preserved archives, to guard against data loss or catastrophic events,
and to facilitate format migration. Any copies exchanged or created
under this section must be handled with at least the same level of
access restrictions as the original holdings, and no publicly
accessible distribution beyond what is otherwise permitted by law or
this Act shall be made from such replicated copies.

### Section 5. Controlled Digital Lending and Interlibrary Loan.

A qualifying library or archive at an accredited educational
institution may lend digitized copies of books or other works in its
collection to patrons of other educational or research institutions
via controlled digital lending and authenticated interlibrary loan
systems. Notwithstanding the rights of the copyright owner, such
digital lending shall be permissible so long as (i) the lending
library and any receiving library together do not allow more
concurrent borrowers of a work than the total number of physical or
legally purchased copies of that work that the lending library (or a
consortium of participating libraries) owns, and (ii) digital copies
lent are made unavailable to borrowers (e.g. automatically “checked
in”) once the loan period expires. Libraries engaging in such lending
must implement technological protections to prevent retention or
further distribution of the digital copy by the borrower beyond the
loan period. For the avoidance of doubt, interlibrary loan of digital
materials under these conditions is explicitly authorized, and the
temporary electronic transfer of a copy to a borrowing institution or
patron as part of such loan does not constitute infringement.

### Section 6. Limitation on Statutory Damages.

In any action for copyright infringement, no award of statutory
damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) or enhanced damages shall be made,
unless the copyright owner had, prior to the commencement of the
infringement, deposited a copy of the allegedly infringed work with a
designated cultural archive. A timely deposit of a work with the
Library of Congress or another designated cultural archive shall
preserve the right to seek statutory damages; failure to so deposit
shall limit the copyright owner to remedies of actual damages and
injunctive relief for that work. This section does not alter the
availability of statutory damages in cases of willful commercial
piracy unrelated to preservation or lending activities protected by
this Act.

### Section 7. Deposit Upon Corporate Dissolution or Bankruptcy.

As a condition of the discharge of debts in bankruptcy or the legal
dissolution of any corporation or entity that owns copyrights in
creative works, any such works (in which the entity holds exclusive
rights) must be deposited in a designated cultural archive. The
appointed bankruptcy trustee or responsible corporate officer shall
arrange to transfer a copy of the work(s), along with any available
source files or metadata, to the designated archive. Such deposit
ensures the works can be preserved and, where appropriate, made
available for research or public interest uses under this Act or other
applicable law. This requirement may not be waived or modified by the
bankruptcy court.


## Why We Need the Cultural Continuity and Preservation Act

### When Preservation Collides with Copyright Law

Digital technology has made it possible to save our cultural heritage
in ways previously unimaginable – but it has also made preservation
inseparable from copying, which brings it into conflict with copyright
law.  Under current U.S. law, libraries and archives have only narrow
privileges to copy works for preservation.  Section 108 of the
Copyright Act (enacted in the 1970s and modestly updated in 1998)
allows at most three copies of an item for preservation, and even
those copies come with strict conditions . For published works, a
library can legally make a preservation copy only to replace one that
is already damaged, deteriorating, lost, or in an obsolete format –
and only after determining that no replacement can be bought at a fair
price. Any digital copy made under Section 108 cannot be made
available to the public outside the library’s premises , effectively
barring remote research use. These limitations, while well-
intentioned in an earlier era, now severely constrain preservation at
scale. In practice, they prevent libraries from proactively digitizing
works before they fall apart, and from providing access to those
digital surrogates even for scholarly research.

Outside of Section 108, libraries and archives must rely on the
general doctrine of “fair use” (17 U.S.C. §107) to justify
preservation copying or digital lending. Fair use can be a powerful
defense, but it is notoriously unpredictable. Whether a given use is
deemed “fair” is determined case by case, often only after costly
litigation – an uncertainty that memory institutions find
chilling. For example, when Google undertook mass digitization of
books for its Google Books search database, it was sued by authors and
publishers.  Ultimately, a court ruled that Google’s actions were fair
use because the digitization was transformative (enabling text search
and snippet displays rather than reading the books
end-to-end). Similarly, a consortium of research libraries prevailed
in Authors Guild v. HathiTrust, which held that libraries’
digitization of books to enable full-text search and access for
print-disabled readers was a lawful fair use. But by contrast, when
the Internet Archive, a nonprofit digital library, scanned books and
lent out full-text digital copies to patrons under a controlled
digital lending system, publishers sued – and the courts this time
found no fair use. The Internet Archive’s program was judged
non-transformative (since the ebooks served the same purpose as the
originals) and harmful to the publishers’ ebook market, and thus an
infringement.

In other words, providing full digital access to copyrighted books,
even on a one-to- one “owned-to-loaned” basis, did not survive the
fair use test in that case. The stark divergence between the Google
Books decision and the Hachette v. Internet Archive outcome highlights
the shaky ground on which preservation and library access currently
stand – hinging on nuanced interpretations of “transformative use” and
market harm that can swing either way.

The result is a climate of fear and uncertainty for libraries. On one
hand, they feel a mission-driven imperative to digitize and safeguard
cultural materials (especially as print copies age or “born-digital”
works become inaccessible due to obsolete formats). On the other hand,
they risk legal liability if a court later disagrees with their view
of fair use. The Internet Archive’s experience is a cautionary tale:
after the adverse judgment, it had to sharply curtail its lending of
books from major publishers, complying with an agreement to remove
those digital books from its library on publishers’ request. I am left
wondering how many smaller libraries or archives, lacking the
resources of Google or the Internet Archive, have refrained from
important preservation projects due to the looming specter of
litigation. Fair use, as a safety valve, has proven to be an
insufficient and unreliable foundation for systematic preservation and
access.

Indeed, even judges and lawyers acknowledge that the multi-factor fair
use test yields “highly unpredictable results” in practice. When the
legality of core library activities is so uncertain, those activities
tend to be abandoned or never attempted in the first place.

### Enforcement Over Memory

Current copyright policy tilts heavily toward enforcement and control,
while doing little to affirmatively protect our cultural memory. The
law provides copyright holders with strong tools to enforce their
rights – from injunctions and impoundment, to statutory damages as
high as $150,000 per work for willful infringement. Such potent
remedies, meant to deter piracy, can also intimidate libraries and
educators. The prospect of crushing damages makes even good-faith
preservation efforts a risky proposition if there’s any doubt about
permission. Meanwhile, there is no corresponding legal mandate to
ensure that works remain available for posterity. Once a book or media
work goes out of print or a publisher shuts down, the public is
effectively at the mercy of rightsholders’ whims: the work can
languish inaccessible, with no law requiring that a copy be archived
for future generations. Our policy has prioritized “rights management”
over stewardship of the knowledge commons – emphasizing private
control and revenue protection at the expense of longevity of
information.

Libraries and archivists have long argued that this balance is
wrong. Archivists emphasize that copyright law should “encourage
enhanced ease of compliance rather than increasingly punitive
enforcement measures.”  In other words, it should be easy for
institutions to do the right thing in preserving materials, instead of
easy for rightsholders to threaten exorbitant penalties. Likewise,
preservationists note that the law “must be changed to allow for a
reasonable number of preservation copies to be made,” and that making
copies without allowing any use is economically unfeasible for
archives. Yet reforms have lagged. Section 108’s library exceptions
have not kept up with the challenges of the digital era, and efforts
to update them have stalled amid pressure from publishers who view any
expansion as a threat to their control. Fair use, for its part, is an
after-the-fact defense that provides no certainty in advance – a
gamble that many institutions (and their legal advisors or insurers)
are unwilling to take.

The result is that our society’s “memory institutions” – libraries,
archives, museums – operate in a legal gray zone. They are tasked with
preserving knowledge but are often prohibited from using the very
tools that make preservation viable at scale. Meanwhile, copyright
owners, in pursuit of enforcement, sometimes ignore the broader public
interest in having works preserved and accessible for research. A
telling perspective came from the recent controlled digital lending
fight: library advocates argued that lending scanned books one at a
time is fundamentally the same as traditional library lending and
poses no new harm to publishers. Libraries have already paid for the
books in their collections and invest heavily in digitization for
preservation. Nevertheless, the hard-line stance of some rightsholders
– backed by current law – is that even this time-honored library
practice should be stopped if not explicitly allowed. In the status
quo, the balance is clear: when in doubt, err on the side of
enforcement. Unfortunately, “when in doubt, do nothing” is the message
that many librarians receive, to the detriment of our collective
memory.

### A Long Overdue Right to Preserve and Share

It is against this backdrop that I propose this Cultural Continuity
and Preservation Act. This Act is an attempt to correct the imbalance,
to carve out space in the law where libraries, archives, and
educational institutions can fulfill their mission to preserve and
provide knowledge, without constantly looking over their shoulders for
a lawsuit. What would this law accomplish? In essence, it provides a
clear statutory right for preservation and controlled sharing, coupled
with incentives for rightsholders to cooperate in the archival
process. Its key provisions include:

- Explicit Preservation Rights for Libraries and Archives: The Act
  grants qualifying libraries, archives, and .edu institutions an
  affirmative license to reproduce copyrighted works they own for
  preservation and research purposes. This means librarians would no
  longer have to twist themselves into knots trying to fit routine
  preservation copying into a narrow §108 exception or pray that a court
  deems it “fair use.” They could digitize aging print materials,
  migrate content from obsolete formats (think of old CDs, DVDs, or
  software on floppy disks), and make backup copies, secure in the
  knowledge that the law explicitly permits it. Importantly, they could
  also provide controlled access to these preserved works for
  researchers – for example, letting authenticated patrons view a
  scanned book on a secure platform – without fear that doing so is a
  copyright violation.

- Secure Synchronization Between Archives: The Act facilitates the
  creation of redundant, geographically distributed archives by allowing
  secure sharing of collections between institutions. In practical
  terms, a university library could transmit a set of digital files to
  another trusted archive for safekeeping, or a consortium of museums
  could maintain a synchronized digital repository. This is crucial for
  disaster preparedness: no single point of failure should wipe out
  humanity’s records.  Under current law, copying a work to send to
  another archive could be seen as an infringing distribution; under the
  new Act, it is a protected preservation activity. Archives can thus
  cooperate to keep multiple safe copies without legal barriers.

- Controlled Digital Lending and Interlibrary Loan: Recognizing the
  reality of modern library services, the Act expressly legalizes
  controlled digital lending (CDL) and authenticated interlibrary loans
  of digital materials. This would codify the principle that if a
  library owns a book, it should be able to lend it either physically or
  digitally, so long as only one patron at a time per copy is able to
  read it. The Act also extends this to interlibrary loan, meaning a
  student at one university could borrow an e-copy of a book from
  another university’s library through secure systems. By explicitly
  permitting CDL, the law would remove the current ambiguity and let
  libraries serve remote learners and researchers more effectively,
  while still respecting the one-copy-one-user balance that protects
  authors’ markets.

- Limiting Damages When Archives Are Cut Off: One especially
  innovative aspect of the proposal is the condition tying statutory
  damages to archival deposit. Under the Act, copyright owners who fail
  to deposit copies of their works with a designated archive would
  forfeit the right to claim statutory or triple damages in court. They
  could still sue an infringer for actual losses, but they wouldn’t be
  eligible for the hefty awards that often make lawsuits so
  threatening. This creates a powerful incentive for publishers and
  authors to voluntarily submit their works to an official archive
  (perhaps the Library of Congress or another trusted repository) soon
  after publication. It’s a “carrot and stick” approach: cooperate in
  preservation and retain your full legal remedies; refuse to cooperate
  and you limit yourself to actual damages if someone infringes. In
  effect, the law would say that if a rightsholder isn’t willing to help
  ensure a work’s longevity, they shouldn’t later reap outsized awards
  for infringement.

- Rescuing Works from Corporate Bankruptcy: Finally, the Act addresses
  a critical gap: what happens to copyrighted materials when companies
  go under. We’ve seen instances of media or tech companies folding,
  with their archives and intellectual property locked away or sold off,
  sometimes to entities that do nothing with them. The Act would require
  that when a company holding copyrights is dissolved without a clear
  successor to keep exploiting those works, copies must be turned over
  to a cultural archive. This ensures that “orphaned” works – which
  might otherwise disappear into legal limbo – are preserved and
  potentially made available for research or eventual public use. It is
  a safeguard against the outright loss of creative works due to
  corporate misfortune.  Think of it as an analogue to how physical
  artifacts might go to a museum if no owner can care for them; here,
  cultural archives become the repository of last resort for our digital
  and literary heritage.  Together, these measures would realign
  copyright law with its constitutional purpose: to promote the progress
  of knowledge and culture. We often forget that copyright is not only
  about protecting authors’ incomes, but also about ensuring that
  creative works benefit the public in the long run. By fortifying the
  right to preserve and share (in a controlled manner), the Cultural
  Continuity and Preservation Act would make clear that libraries and
  archives are not intruders on copyright, but indispensable partners in
  its grand bargain.

In my view, this Act is long overdue. It is a restrained, reasonable
step – not a radical overturning of authors’ rights, but a
recalibration to stop treating libraries like potential pirates. It
acknowledges that enduring access to knowledge is as important as
short-term access to markets. Right now, our policies implicitly favor
enforcement over memory, policing access more vigorously than we
conserve information. It’s time to change that. We need a statutory
right to preserve and interoperate, a guarantee that the libraries of
the 21st century can carry forward the torch that libraries have held
for millennia: the torch of collective memory. The Cultural Continuity
and Preservation Act would help ensure that when future generations
seek the literature, art, and knowledge of our era, they will find an
intact record – not a digital black hole. It’s a policy change that
honors both the rights of creators and the rights of the public to
remember and learn, striking a long-missing balance in our copyright
system.
